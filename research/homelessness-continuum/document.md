# The Continuum Portland Can't See

## What each person needs, in what order, how to count who is where, and where the gaps are

*Portland Civic Lab · September 2026 · Working draft for review*

*Every figure below traces to a public document listed in [sources.md](sources.md), cited as [id]. Judgments are ours and labeled. What we could not verify is in section 14. Sharon Meieran's plan documents are cited as primary sources for her proposals; the design in section 5 is ours, built on her work, the national frameworks, and the trial evidence, and was drafted by three independent reviewers, scored by three judges, synthesized, and then challenged claim by claim.*

---

**Answer in brief.** Portland cannot see its continuum because it counts contacts, placements, and beds rather than people at stages, and because the institutions that hold the cleanest data (the jail, the hospitals, the state hospital) are not connected to the count. We propose a fourteen-stage continuum in six phases (prevent, find, stabilize, shelter and bridge, house, sustain) under four rules: a person is in one physical stage at a time; a referral is not an exit until the receiving site records an arrival; stabilization is a first placement and never a precondition for a lease; and every transition carries a named lead, a next place already held, and a confirmed handoff within seven days. Twelve cohorts each get an evidence-graded pathway through those stages. A six-question first-door protocol lets any responder, whether outreach, police, EMS, an emergency department, or a jail, name the right first stage from what they can observe, and three acuity lanes set the intensity of support without gating any later door. Housing First is the rule for people with serious mental illness, co-occurring disorders, frequent jail contact, and young adults with mental illness. It works with conditions for families (the condition is a permanent subsidy), for any program that claims the name (fidelity), and for the highest-acuity few in a scarce market (master leasing and a risk fund). It is not by itself an answer for substance use, for someone who cannot consent today, for someone too sick for a shelter, as a time-limited subsidy, as compulsory treatment, or in a region with no units. Counting each stage needs seven fields that already exist in HMIS, eight rules, and nine headline numbers. Today none of the fourteen stages is fully counted; ten are partly counted and four (diversion, institutional in-reach, bridge and recovery housing, and the retention loop) are not counted at all. Those four are also where the stabilize-first lane has no floor. The design has legal limits, governance limits, and gaming risks, all kept in view in section 9.

## 1. Why the region cannot see its own continuum

Multnomah County publishes a by-name list, a shelter dashboard, a Point-in-Time count, a Built for Zero chronic count, and, since December 2025, twelve action-plan indicators. None of them answers the question a field worker, a budget analyst, or a voter needs answered: **how many people are at each stage between the sidewalk and a lease, and are they moving?**

The reasons are specific and documented, mostly by the county itself.

- **The list counts service contacts, not people at a stage.** The by-name list adds anyone who touches shelter, outreach, a housing program, or a day center, and "outflow" includes people who "became inactive (didn't show up in our data)" [multco-bnl-release]. The published inactivity threshold in days is not stated on any county page we could fetch.
- **The county graded its own list 20 of 29 in October 2024** against the Built for Zero quality standard, and the nine failures are exactly the ones that decide whether stage counts are possible: no mapped outreach coverage, no confidence that 90% of unsheltered people are on the list, fewer than 90% of providers reporting, no way to track people who have not consented, no data-quality protocol with submission timelines, and no homeless/housed status field with the date it last changed [multco-bfz-scorecard].
- **Shelter exits are mostly unknown.** In the county's FY2025 review of 31 adult shelters, 2,800 of 5,213 exits, 54%, had no recorded destination; 93% of those were from congregate sites [multco-shelter-review].
- **Outreach is counted outside the system of record.** FY2026 outreach engagements were pulled from a separate mapping tool "because not all providers log engagements in HMIS" [shs-q4-fy26].
- **The institutions with perfect data are not connected.** Jail publishes no housing status at booking or release destination [mcso-data]; Unity Center publishes no discharge-destination data; the 2018 match of jail, Medicaid, and homeless-services records that found 1,371 people touching all three systems has never been repeated [fuse-multco].
- **The 2025 count changed method mid-series.** Multnomah added 5,090 people "presumed unsheltered" from its own list to the 1,822 surveyed on the street, which is most of the 61% regional increase [pit-2025].

The result is a system that can report placements and contacts but cannot say whether a given person, or a given thousand people, are stuck, and where. That is Meieran's central charge: the county "measures processes and activities," which "produces false baselines" and "masks failure" [meieran-plan]. Her by-name-list document puts the test in one sentence: "If the number of people living unsheltered is not going down, the system is failing, regardless of how many services are delivered" [meieran-bnl].

## 2. What people need first, by who they are

The site already sorts people into three broad groups and twelve operational cohorts. What it does not do is say, with evidence, what each needs first and what happens when the order is wrong. The strongest research does, and it is more decisive than the local debate suggests.

| Cohort | What they need first | Evidence and grade | The common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First-time, economic** (~80% of shelter users in the Kuhn–Culhane typology; 38% of Portland street-survey respondents said this was their first time) | Money and speed: a one-time payment inside days, plus light housing search. Not shelter, not an assessment queue. | Chicago emergency assistance: 76% less shelter entry within six months, quasi-experimental [evans-2016]. NYC Homebase RCT: shelter nights 32 → 10 [homebase-rct]. Vancouver cash-transfer RCT: 88 fewer days homeless per year [new-leaf]. | The payment arrives after the eviction, or the household is routed into shelter and a queue built for chronic cases. |
| **Episodic, moderate behavioral-health need** (~10% of shelter users) | A unit with a subsidy plus intensive case management that does not expire on a calendar, and same-week outpatient access. | At Home/Chez Soi moderate-needs arm, RCT [chez-soi-2015]. Time-limited rapid rehousing decays: −12.9 points instability at day 120, −2.4 at three years [ssvf-2025]. Critical Time Intervention at transitions: homelessness ~5× lower after hospital discharge, RCT [cti-rct]. | Bare, time-limited rapid rehousing with no clinical link; or over-placing in supportive housing that someone else needed. |
| **Chronic with serious mental illness** (49% of Multnomah's count meets the federal chronic definition; 26% of respondents report serious mental illness) | An apartment now with no treatment precondition, plus an ACT team for high-need cases; a CTI worker assigned before any hospital discharge. | Housing First with ACT: 71% vs 29% of time stably housed over 24 months, RCT [aubry-2016]; with intensive case management, 73% vs 32% [chez-soi-2015]; the advantage narrowed but held to year six in Toronto [chez-soi-2019]. ACT vs standard case management: 37% greater reduction in homelessness across six RCTs [act-meta]. | Treatment-first: requiring stabilization or sobriety before a unit, which the trials show delays housing by months (73 versus 220 days) without clinical benefit. |
| **Chronic with severe substance use** (30% of adults with data report a chronic SUD; 214 of 372 deaths in 2024 were overdoses) | Survival and engagement in that order: same-day low-threshold buprenorphine or methadone, contingency management for stimulants, naloxone where the person sleeps, housing not conditioned on stopping. Recovery housing for those who choose abstinence. | Contingency management is the only psychosocial intervention with durable abstinence effects across 50 RCTs [cm-meta]. Sobering center $264 per encounter vs $649 ED [sobering-sf]. Compulsory commitment: no benefit found, possible harm [commitment-review]. | Sobriety as the ticket to housing; forced treatment into beds that do not exist; detox with no next place to go. |
| **Co-occurring SMI and SUD** | One team, one plan: Housing First with an integrated ACT team that treats both, with MOUD and contingency management available. | Pathways RCT (serious mental illness required; nine in ten with a recorded substance disorder or history): earlier and more stable housing without prerequisites [pathways-2004]; no difference in self-reported substance use at 48 months [pathways-2006]. | Serial systems: mental health says get sober first, addiction treatment says get stable first, housing says do both first. |
| **Families with children** (1,200+ families on the county family waitlist) | Same-day non-congregate placement to keep children in school, then a long-term subsidy as fast as possible. | Family Options RCT: a subsidy cut homelessness or doubling-up by half and shelter stays by 75% at 37 months; rapid rehousing showed no stability gain at 37 months [family-options]. | Treating rapid rehousing as the family solution because it is cheapest; rule-heavy transitional housing. |
| **Unaccompanied youth** (928 counted tri-county, 899 aged 18–24) | A youth-specific safe place within 24–72 hours, never the adult shelter, plus a safety-screened family-reconnection conversation, then a subsidy with youth-tailored support. | Housing First under the adult model for 18–24-year-olds with mental illness: 65% vs 31% of days stably housed, post-hoc RCT subgroup [chez-soi-youth]; youth adaptations untested. Host homes: practice reports only. | Routing an 18-year-old into the adult queue, where exploitation is the harm. |
| **Domestic violence and trafficking survivors** (56% of those with known status were homeless because of it) | Safety and survivor choice: a confidential non-congregate placement the same day, a mobile advocate, and flexible cash usable for rent, transport, or relocation. | Washington DVHF evaluation: better housing stability, safety, and mental health at six months, holding at two years, quasi-experimental [dvhf-2022]. | Congregate DV shelter as the only door; generic rapid rehousing with documentation rules the survivor cannot meet. |
| **Medically fragile, elderly, disabled** (average age at death among homeless Portlanders: 48) | Never a discharge to the street: medical respite arranged before discharge with a housing worker attached at admission, then housing with in-home supports. | Respite discharge halves 90-day readmission odds, propensity-weighted [kertesz-2009]. Housing plus case management: 29% fewer hospitalizations, RCT [sadowski-2009]. | Discharge to shelter or self-care, which is how 73% of homeless patients leave Oregon hospitals [discharge-taskforce]. |
| **Justice-involved, frequent public-safety contacts** (the 2018 match found 1,371 people in jail, Medicaid, and homeless data at once) | Reach-in before release or immediately after, a held bridge bed, and supportive housing within about 90–120 days, not a waitlist. | Just in Reach: 82% housed 12 months after placement, 24 fewer jail days, propensity-matched [just-in-reach]. Denver: 27% fewer jail days over three years intent to treat, 81% of those housed retained at two years, RCT [urban-denver]. NYC FUSE II: 40% fewer jail days, about 91% fewer shelter days, matched [fuse-ii-nyc]. | Release to the street, then re-arrest for survival behavior; supervision conditions that void the tenancy. |
| **Vehicle-dwelling working poor** (vehicles are the most common unsheltered location in Clackamas and Washington counties) | A legal, safe place to park with sanitation so the vehicle is not towed, plus a navigator and a small cash or rapid-rehousing package. | San Diego safe parking: housing exits at four times the rate of bridge shelter, mixed-methods [safe-parking-sd]. | Towing the vehicle, which turns a working, self-sheltered person into an unsheltered, unemployed one. |
| **Service-refusing, long-term encampment** | Find the specific refusal and remove it: an offer that takes the pet, the partner, and the belongings, is non-congregate, and is made repeatedly by the same small team. | Sustained small-team outreach produced faster acceptance in three LA encampment resolutions, mixed-methods [abt-encampments]. Repeated involuntary displacement of people who inject drugs modeled to raise deaths, simulation [barocas-2023]. | Calling a congregate, no-pets, no-partner bed an "offer," recording the decline as "service-refusing," then clearing the camp. |

Two cross-cutting findings organize the design below.

**Transitions are where people are lost, and a single named worker who starts before the exit is the best-replicated fix.** Critical Time Intervention is a nine-month, three-phase bridge that begins inside the hospital, jail, or shelter [cti-rct]. Every strong justice-system result depends on the placement being arranged inside the institution [just-in-reach]. Meieran's "Intensive Care Teams" and "warm handoff plus follow-up within 7 days" are the same idea in local language [meieran-bh].

**Intensity should follow acuity, and the highest-acuity group is small.** Kuhn and Culhane found the chronic 10% of shelter users consumed half of all shelter days [kuhn-culhane]. Meieran's three-lane budget makes the same point in dollars: 14,000–17,000 people a year at $5,000–6,000, 4,000–5,000 at $18,000–24,000, and 900–1,200 at $45,000–65,000, on a flat $250M [meieran-acuity]. "We need to stop pretending everyone costs the same."

## 3. What Meieran proposes, and what the peer systems share

A note on weight. This memo leans on one local author more than on any other, and she is a candidate for county chair. We use her documents for three things no other local source provides: stage-by-stage targets, a three-lane acuity model with volumes and costs, and a by-name-list specification. Her volumes and costs are planning assumptions, not measured outcomes, and are labeled that way wherever they appear on the site. The design itself rests on trials, federal standards, statutes, and the county's own reports; where it departs from her proposals, section 5 says so.

Sharon Meieran, an emergency physician and county commissioner from 2017 to 2024, published a linked set of turnaround documents between January and February 2026 and is running for county chair on them. We read 46 documents; the plan, its nine linked sub-documents, her 2024 expert-group report on a substance-use continuum, and the 2023 FUSE data match she sponsored are the substantive ones [meieran-docs].

Her homelessness-to-housing continuum has four pillars plus a coordination function: prevention; outreach, identification, intake and assessment; shelter, safety and transition; housing and retention; and "Intensive Care Teams" that own transitions [meieran-plan]. Her behavioral-health plan adds seven components: prevention, outreach and harm reduction, crisis response, two kinds of shelter (crisis stabilization measured in hours to days, transitional behavioral-health shelter measured in days to weeks), treatment managed as an ASAM-style flow, recovery housing, and system flow [meieran-bh]. The acuity document sorts everyone into three lanes: prevent and resolve quickly; supported transition through bridge housing; and stabilize first, then house, with stabilization as the first milestone for the highest-acuity group [meieran-acuity].

What makes her work usable is that every stage carries a number:

| Stage | Meieran's target |
|---|---|
| Prevention | Payment within 5 business days; ≥80–85% still housed at 12 months; ≥20% fewer eviction filings; cost per averted shelter entry below 60 days of shelter |
| Outreach and intake | A verified by-name list within 6 months; ≥90% of unsheltered people on it, audited quarterly; contacted within 7 days; a documented next step within 14 days for ≥60–70% |
| Shelter | Median length of stay falling quarter over quarter, by type; ≥35–50% of exits to permanent housing by model; <20% returns within 6 months; avoidable vacancy <5%; cost per housing exit published by site |
| Housing | ≥90% of placements matched to acuity; retention ≥85% at 6 months, ≥80% at 12, ≥75% at 24, counted against everyone placed |
| Crisis | Time to response; disposition (community, ED, detox, jail); warm handoff completed and follow-up within 7 days; 30- and 90-day repeat crisis |

Her by-name list is deliberately not HMIS. Eight fields: verified name, location, unsheltered yes/no, length of homelessness, immediate barriers, assigned lead, current pathway stage, and last confirmed contact, with one rule: not physically confirmed in 30 days means not counted as unsheltered until re-verified. Daily counts of total unsheltered, inflow split into first-time and returns, outflow to housing, to shelter, and to other with the institution named, returns, and deaths [meieran-bnl].

The peer systems she cites share six features, and the list holds up against the national frameworks: one system owner rather than a convener; a real by-name list used daily; prevention as core infrastructure paid in days; shelter as transition with enforced length-of-stay targets; retention measured at 6, 12, and 24 months; and funding tied to performance [meieran-peers]. Houston's "The Way Home" and Hennepin County's Housing Stability department are the two she names most.

Where we depart from her, we say so in section 5. The main points: her by-name list should be the operational front end of the system of record, not a parallel database, because a separate list recreates the double-counting problem the tri-county region is spending millions to fix; and her "stabilize first" lane needs the guardrail the trials insist on, which is that stabilization is a first placement, not a precondition for a lease.

Two patterns run through the table. **The system is thinnest at exactly the stages the highest-acuity lane needs:** sobering, withdrawal, residential treatment, respite, recovery housing, and institutional discharge. **And the stages that do exist cannot report where people go next.** Shelter exit destinations are unknown for half of exits; outreach lives in a side tool; jail and hospital exits are invisible. That is why the continuum cannot be drawn from today's data, and it is the design constraint for section 7.

## 5. The continuum: fourteen stages, four rules

A stage in this design is not a program or a building. It is a physical situation a person can be in, defined by a number a worker can take this week: who enters, how they leave, how long it should take, who belongs there, how to count it, and what Portland has and lacks at that stage. If outreach, police, the emergency department, the jail, and the housing system use the same fourteen words, they can hand a person to each other and the count can follow the person.

Four rules hold the stages together. They are the design; everything else is detail.

1. **One person, one physical stage.** A person is in exactly one stage at a time, set by their last verified living situation plus days since contact. Queues and housing matches are overlays and never add to a headcount.
2. **A referral is not an exit.** Someone leaves a stage only when the receiving site records their arrival. Every yes carries a timestamp, so a yes at 2 a.m. with no door to open is a counted event, not an anecdote.
3. **Stabilization is a first placement, never the ticket.** People may pass through sobering, detox, treatment, or respite, but no one has to complete a stage to be offered a lease. The trials show housing works without preconditions [chez-soi-2015] [pathways-2004].
4. **Transitions are where people are lost.** Every institutional exit and every stabilization discharge carries a named lead, a next place already held, and a confirmed handoff within seven days [cti-rct] [just-in-reach].

The stages fall into six phases, left to right: **prevent** (keep the door shut), **find** (know who is where), **stabilize** (hours to weeks), **shelter and bridge** (days to months), **house** (a lease), and **sustain** (stay housed). The Portland figures below are those of section 4 and carry the same citations; "counted" means a published figure exists for who is in the stage now, "partly" means capacity or annual throughput is published but not who is there today, and "not counted" means nobody can say.

**Where this departs from Meieran.** Her four pillars, her three lanes, her stage targets, and her insistence that the by-name list be verified and dated are the backbone of this design [meieran-plan] [meieran-acuity] [meieran-bnl]. Three departures. First, her eight-field by-name list should be the operational front end of the region's system of record, not a parallel database; a second list recreates the double-counting the tri-county region is spending years to remove [multco-bitfocus]. Second, her "stabilize first" lane needs the guardrail the trials insist on: stabilization is a first placement, and the lease offer is made from the stabilization bed, never after a completed program. Third, her assessment stage is an overlay in our count, not a place; a person waiting for a match is counted where they physically are, so the queue can be long without inflating the headcount.

## 6. Who goes where: the first door, three lanes, when Housing First works, and twelve pathways

Most failures in this system are the wrong first door, not the wrong last one. A person in withdrawal routed to a housing assessment, a first-time family routed to the adult chronic queue, a jail release with no worker: each is a first-door error, and each is visible at the scene. So the protocol is six questions, in order, that any responder can answer from what they can see or ask in one breath. Safety, then stabilization, then population, then history. The first draft of these questions was read by an outreach worker, a paramedic, a patrol officer, a jail release desk, an emergency-department social worker, an addiction clinician, and a lawyer, each asked what breaks at 2 a.m.; section 9 lists what they found, and this version carries their fixes.

Six rules every agency signs:

1. **At the scene nothing new is written.** Outreach adds two fields to the contact row at end of shift: the referral result (accepted; declined, with the person's reason; door refused, with the system's reason; no door open at this hour) and arrival status. Police and fire record the same set as a dispatch disposition, matched monthly to the receiving door's admission log; hospitals record it as a discrete discharge-destination field. Entering it into the system of record is the receiving site's job, never the officer's or the nurse's.
2. **Every yes gets a held bed, a ride by a responder authorized for that destination, and an arrival written by the receiving site** as a condition of the bed-night or encounter bill. Until then the person has not left the stage. Where no door can be held at that hour the result is coded no door open: a capacity finding by door and hour, never a decline and never a worker's score.
3. **A named lead within 7 days for anyone verified outside:** the outreach enrollment carrying the most recent date of engagement, one per person across programs, transferable by a logged handoff, with a caseload ceiling and a visible lead-capacity-exhausted state so the gap reads as staffing. Identified is not engaged.
4. **A decline is recorded against the offer, not the person:** the site and the rule that caused it (pets, partner, congregate, sobriety rule, belongings and storage, distance, accessibility, curfew, no ID, a named site's history, not tonight), aggregated nightly with no person key, because a per-person decline log is evidence under the city's camping ordinance [pcc-14a-50-150]. System-side results (no bed, referral-only, no transport, closed at this hour, clinical exclusion, charge ineligibility) are coded separately. Never "service-refusing", and never a warrant.
5. **The lane is provisional at first contact and re-scored at every handoff.** It decides the first door, not eligibility for any later one. At the scene there is one observable split, Lane 3 or not yet; Lane 2 is assigned from history at the first handoff, and a person impaired tonight is not laned until re-approached within 12–24 hours.
6. **Nothing here authorizes detention.** Transport is voluntary unless a peace officer has probable cause of danger to self or others (ORS 426.228) or the person is incapacitated by intoxication in a public place (ORS 430.399: to a treatment or sobering facility, jail only when no facility is available); a qualified mental health professional may request a director's hold (ORS 426.233) [ors-426] [ors-430-399]. Every involuntary transport is coded as such.

### 6.3 When Housing First works, and when it doesn't

Housing First is the best-replicated result in this field for people with serious mental illness and long homelessness. It is also not a treatment for addiction, not a wound clinic, not a plan for a region with no units, and not a substitute for the hours of stabilization a person in psychosis or withdrawal needs before they can say yes to a room. Thirteen cases, three verdicts. Nine of the thirteen evidence strings were handed to independent refuters; every headline direction survived, and the numbers, trial arms, and citations below are the corrected ones (section 9).

| Verdict | Case | The rule | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|

### 6.4 Twelve pathways

Each cohort is an ordered chain of stages with the evidence for that order. What they share is the last stage: someone still checking at month 24. The evidence grade is the strongest study behind the order, not behind every step; where only one step is trial-backed we say so.

- **Recently homeless, economic shock** (RCT [santa-clara-rct]): Prevention → Diversion → Emergency shelter → Rapid rehousing → Housed and followed. Cash inside the 10-day notice clock beats shelter; shelter is a short motel bridge only if diversion fails; the clinical stages are skipped on purpose.
- **Car or RV, working poor** (observational [safe-parking-sd]): Prevention → Verified unsheltered → Assessment and match → Bridge (safe parking) → Rapid rehousing → Housed and followed. Self-sheltered until the vehicle is towed; safe parking with sanitation is the bridge. Locally none exists: the former North Portland Road RV site is now 212 pods with RVs phased out [n-portland-rd-village].
- **Families with children** (RCT [family-options]): Prevention → Diversion → Emergency shelter (same-day, non-congregate, which does not yet exist at night) → Rapid rehousing → Housed and followed. The rapid-rehousing stage must convert to a permanent voucher; retention is where that is verified.
- **Youth and young adults** (RCT for the housing step only [chez-soi-youth]): Diversion (safety-screened family reconnection) → Youth-only shelter within 24–72 hours → Transitional living → A subsidy with support → Housed and followed. Only the housing step is trial-backed, under the adult model; youth-only shelter, transitional living, and host homes are practice judgments.
- **Survivors of violence and trafficking** (quasi-experimental [dvhf-2022]): Prevention (flexible cash) → Confidential non-congregate placement → Flexible-funding rapid rehousing with a mobile advocate → Housed and followed. Records stay in the confidential database and reach the count as flags only [comparable-database]; the first responder records a living situation and nothing else.
- **Severe substance use disorder** (RCT [cm-meta]): Verified unsheltered → Sobering → Withdrawal management → Recovery housing by choice → Supportive housing → Housed and followed. The default is outpatient buprenorphine or methadone with retention, started at whichever door can prescribe, plus a lease not conditioned on abstinence; sobering, withdrawal management, and residential treatment are detours when indicated and chosen, each with the next bed held, because detox or residential without medication raises overdose death from lost tolerance. The continuum still lacks an outpatient treatment stage to count this on (section 9).
- **Serious mental illness** (RCT [aubry-2016]): Verified unsheltered → Crisis stabilization (as a detour when needed) → Supportive housing with ACT → Housed and followed. The shortest evidence-backed path: 71% of time stably housed versus 29% over 24 months in the high-needs arm, an advantage that narrowed but held to year six in Toronto. If hospitalized, the path re-enters through in-reach with a transition worker assigned before discharge.
- **Co-occurring mental illness and substance use** (RCT [pathways-2004] [pathways-2006]): Verified unsheltered → Crisis stabilization → Withdrawal management → Behavioral-health transitional shelter (days to weeks, only while a unit is found) → Supportive housing with an integrated team → Housed and followed. The Pathways trial enrolled people with serious mental illness, nine in ten with a recorded substance disorder or history, and tested immediate housing, not the pre-housing stages; those are held to days.
- **Medically fragile, elderly, disabled** (quasi-experimental [kertesz-2009]): Institutional in-reach (worker attached at admission) → Medical respite, or skilled and long-term care for those respite excludes → Supportive housing or long-term care with in-home supports → Housed and followed. Never a discharge to the street.
- **Chronically homeless but relatively stable** (observational [kuhn-culhane]): Verified unsheltered → Assessment and match within 90 days → Low-barrier shelter or village only while documents and the unit come together → Supportive housing → Housed and followed. The county prices supportive housing at $16,000 per person against $47,000 per shelter bed [multco-shelter-release].
- **Justice-involved, repeat public-safety contacts** (RCT [urban-denver]): Institutional in-reach (the booking interview for most; 120-day reach-in for supervision cases) → A held bridge or interim bed → Supportive housing within about 90–120 days → Housed and followed. The studies tested bridge-then-housing, not jail-door-to-lease; sobering or detox are detours on release day, never conditions.
- **Veterans** (RCT [hud-vash-rct]): Verified unsheltered → Assessment and match (the VA's Community Resource and Referral Center) → Bridge (Supportive Services for Veteran Families or Grant and Per Diem beds) → Supportive housing (a HUD-VASH voucher with VA case management) → Housed and followed. The randomized trial of 460 veterans found 25% more days housed and 36% fewer days homeless over three years than standard care. Tri-county counted 771 homeless veterans in 2025, up 36% [pit-2025]; the VA call line answers 24/7 [va-call-center]; the Portland center publishes no hours [va-portland-locations].
- **Service-refusing, unsafe encampment** (observational [abt-encampments]): Verified unsheltered (same small team, repeatedly, with a clinical line to tell refusal from untreated psychosis or withdrawal) → Non-congregate bridge that takes the pet, the partner, and the belongings → Supportive housing → Housed and followed. The decline is recorded against the offer, never the person.

## 7. Counting each bucket without a brittle process

The count has to survive three things: workers who will not fill in forty fields, providers who report late or not at all, and people who move between jail, hospital, shelter, and street without telling anyone. The design principle is Meieran's ("fields redesigned around pathways, not paperwork") and Built for Zero's ("data built into the delivery of services") [meieran-bnl] [bfz-standards]. The specifics below are ours, drawn from the HMIS data standard the region already runs and from the communities that have made this work.

**Seven fields, all of which already exist in HMIS.**

1. Person key: name and date of birth, hashed into one regional ID. The tri-county move to a single Bitfocus system makes this possible for the first time [multco-bitfocus].
2. Contact date and source: which program saw the person and when. Every funded touch writes one row.
3. Living situation at contact, collapsed into a stage: street; vehicle; overnight shelter; 24-hour or alternative shelter; detox or residential treatment; jail; hospital or psychiatric; transitional or recovery housing; housed with subsidy; housed without; deceased. **This single field is the stage count.**
4. Stage entry, exit, destination, and housing move-in date, so placements and returns are dated events, not inferences.
5. Cohort flags already collected: disabling condition, veteran, household composition, age, domestic violence via the separate confidential database. Chronic status is derived from contact history, never asked.
6. Referral event and result: type, date, accepted or rejected by whom, result date. This is the funnel step the site's plan calls "measure treatment, not vibes," and it makes bed availability a by-product of the transaction.
7. Capacity per site per day: licensed, funded, staffed, occupied, open tonight, reported by the operator as a condition of billing the bed-night, not into a separate registry. Voluntary registries go stale within hours; Oregon's own bed-coordination center produced about 30 activations in 16 months for roughly $7M [ww-obcc].

**Eight rules.**

- **The worker at the point of contact enters it, inside the system of record.** Never a side spreadsheet. Agencies outside HMIS (jail, emergency departments, Portland Street Response, treatment providers) contribute fields 1–3 only, through a monthly hashed match under a data-use agreement, the method HUD's own toolkit documents and Multnomah used once in 2018 [hud-data-toolkit] [fuse-multco].
- **A stage is "last known situation plus days since contact," never a guess.** Each person is counted in the stage of their most recent contact and shown in a staleness band: 0–30 days is *known*, 31–90 is *presumed*, over 90 is *inactive*. Charlotte-Mecklenburg auto-exits outreach and coordinated-entry records at 90 days [mecklenburg-one-number]; Meieran's rule is 30 days for unsheltered status [meieran-bnl]. The county should publish its threshold. Today it does not.
- **Degrade gracefully.** A missing living situation defaults to *unknown*, never to unsheltered or housed. A provider that stops reporting is flagged as a coverage hole with its last date, rather than silently shrinking the count. The 2025 count's "presumed unsheltered" assumption should be a published parameter, not a baked-in method change.
- **Self-check every period.** Active this week equals active last week plus inflow minus outflow; publish the error and hold it under Built for Zero's 15% standard [bfz-standards]. A week that fails is published as failing.
- **Count people, not enrollments or calls.** One ID per person; institutional stays are a stage, not an exit, so nobody is simultaneously "housed" and "in jail"; returns from inactive count as inflow, not as new.
- **Separate the who-is-here list from the who-gets-what queue.** The count is contact-driven. Prioritization assessments are optional enrichment and never a precondition for being counted, which is the lesson of the VI-SPDAT's retirement and the bias findings that included Multnomah [vi-spdat-retire].
- **Close the loop from the institutions that already have perfect data.** Jail release date and housing status at booking, emergency and psychiatric registrations, and Medical Examiner deaths flow back monthly as stage transitions with a reason. Domicile Unknown already checks HMIS for ambiguous cases; the feed should run the other way too [domicile-unknown].
- **Publish weekly as a fixed-column table** (stage × staleness band × cohort flag × county) with the reliability percentage and the provider coverage list. Keep the monthly number as the official one so the weekly view is operational, not political.

**What the county and the city measure today.** The two governments share a plan and a set of indicators and dispute each other's numbers in public; the city's shelter and outreach data live in city systems with no published link to the county's, and the 2024 audit's two findings (no single adult entry point, no real-time bed availability) are still open [city-audit-2024] [opb-data]. Washington County has met the Built for Zero quality standard for chronic single adults since 2020; Houston runs one system with one owner [bfz-standards] [meieran-peers].

## 9. What the critique found

Before publication the design was challenged twice. First, twelve load-bearing factual claims were handed to independent reviewers with instructions to refute them against primary documents. Eleven held. One was wrong and is corrected throughout: the deflection center's sobering stations are not police-only. Since June 24, 2025 they accept referrals from Portland Street Response, Fire CHAT, Project Respond, and named outreach and peer teams; there is still no walk-in or self-referral, and the deflection program itself remains a law-enforcement or district-attorney referral [multco-sobering-services] [deflection-annual]. Five precision fixes came with it: the 38% first-time and 56% domestic-violence figures are tri-county, not Multnomah [pit-2025]; the 155 withdrawal and 683 residential beds were projections for the third quarter of 2025, and the current licensed counts are 139 and 639 [oha-facility-study]; HMIS element 4.12 is collected only in outreach, night-by-night shelter, coordinated-entry, and services-only projects, so entry-exit shelters need 3.10 and 3.12 instead; element 4.20 records a referral result but has no arrival field, which is why the design adds one local field [hmis-dictionary]; and Hooper's 17% placement rate is a share of the 56% recommended for residential care, not of all patients [ccc-beds].

Second, the six questions, the five rules, the three lanes, and the thirteen Housing First cases were handed to seven readers asked what breaks at 2 a.m.: a county-contracted outreach worker, a paramedic, a patrol officer, a jail release desk, an emergency-department social worker, an addiction clinician, and a lawyer, each checking against the county's own pages, EMS protocols, statutes, and program offers. They found sixty-odd breaks and the design carries their fixes. Four refuters (families, medical respite, time-limited subsidies, and a region with no units) did not complete and those four strings stand unrefuted rather than verified.

And since June 1, 2026, defendants on listed low-level charges found unable to aid and assist are ineligible for the state hospital and routed to community restoration under the county mental health program, many with a same-day release from jail on a dismissal; the three metro district attorneys say no agency publishes where they sleep [mink-bowman-das] [mink-bowman-order].

**Legal limits on a shared count.** Victim-service providers may not enter identifying information into HMIS even hashed, so for the cohort that 56% of respondents name as the cause of their homelessness the one-person-one-stage rule can only be enforced with aggregate flags [comparable-database]. Substance-use treatment records need written patient consent under 42 CFR Part 2, whose 2024 rule took effect in February 2026, so the detox and residential match rates need a consent workflow or will read mostly unknown for years [part-2]. Hospital diagnosis flags for homelessness had 28% sensitivity in a linkage study, so a discharge-to-street rate built on them reports a third of the truth. The admission question already exists: federal quality reporting has required a housing-instability screen for every admitted adult since 2024 [cms-sdoh]; what is missing is asking it in the emergency department and linking the answer to a recorded destination, as California's SB 1152 requires. The county already has an inactive rule: the January 2026 Street Outreach Handbook defines inactive as three months with no service transaction plus three documented contact attempts and directs quarterly auto-exit, so the 30- and 90-day bands sit on top of a rule that exists and the change is publishing it [street-outreach-handbook]. The jail's three record systems (booking, recognizance, and Corrections Health) have no living-situation field, and two of the three owners are not the county board. Deaths cannot be reported monthly as designed, because homeless status at death is determined annually with a 12-month lag [domicile-unknown].

**Nobody owns it yet.** The single system of record does not exist: the three counties chose one vendor in July 2025 and said full rollout will take a few more years, and the city's shelter and outreach data live in city systems with no published link, so the first years of this design are a migration and every published trend is a series break [multco-bitfocus]. Tri-county in name, Multnomah in data: Washington County has met the Built for Zero quality standard for chronic single adults since 2020 and Clackamas runs its own coordinated access, so a regional stage table built from one county's data will be read as that county's problem exported [bfz-standards]. Federal money now scores the opposite of the fidelity rule: the July 2025 executive order directs HUD to end support for Housing First and require treatment participation, and the $37.7M grant that funds the region's supportive housing is in litigation over exactly this [eo-14321].

**How the headline number could mislead.** Switching the official count from the list (14,361 people in the January 2025 release, anyone who touched a service) to a physically verified 30-day count would land near the 1,822 the 2025 street survey found, in the middle of a city-county fight over inflated data and a chair's race in which the plan's author is a candidate; publish both series side by side for at least a year. A verified count falls whenever verification capacity falls, and the county has cut its navigators to zero, so under this design every such cut reads as outflow and the balance check passes; publish coverage and reliability beside the headline, and add unsheltered-to-housing arrivals as a share of all outflow. Yes-to-arrival within 24 hours is red by construction until the 47-bed center opens in late 2027, and a metric that penalizes a 2 a.m. yes with no door removes the incentive to log the yes; report it as a capacity finding with the missing door named. And diversion can become the screen-out federal coordinated-entry rules forbid: a stage judged on how many first-time presenters enter shelter anyway rewards keeping people out of beds, when the evidence came from cash offered to people who asked for help [hud-ce-notice].

**Where police belong.**

- Police are the most common first contact at night and the only responder who can transport under ORS 430.399 or take custody under ORS 426.228. Those two statutes are the whole of their authority here.
- They open three doors tonight: sobering (for the intoxicated adult who can agree), Unity (intoxication or psychiatric emergency, adults), and an emergency department. They cannot open a shelter, a family placement, a youth bed, or a housing assessment at 2 a.m., and the protocol does not pretend they can.
- Their record is a dispatch disposition (offered X, declined with reason, transported to Y, arrival time), matched monthly to the receiving door's log. They never enter HMIS and never write a warrant into a shared record.
- Camping enforcement is a day-unit procedure after a documented, usable offer; it is not the model contact. A warrant hit at the scene means jail, coded as door refused, not as the person's decline.
- Police presence at any stage after the first door (assessment, detox, treatment, shelter intake, housing) is a sign the system failed a step, and is counted that way.

## 11. When someone says no

Most refusals are refusals of a specific offer, or come from someone not in a state to accept any offer tonight. The law draws two lines, danger to self or others and incapacitation in public, and below them nobody can make anyone go anywhere. The protocol for a no is therefore an order of questions, a re-approach clock, a decline recorded against the offer rather than the person, and enforcement last.

1. **First ask who is refusing.** A no from someone intoxicated, in withdrawal, or in psychosis is not a decline; it is a deferral. Re-approach within 12–24 hours. If the person is incapacitated in public, the law already says where they go: a treatment or sobering facility, an emergency department, or Unity, and jail only when nothing will admit them. If they are a danger to themselves or others, an officer can take custody. Below those two lines, nobody can make anyone go anywhere, and the protocol says so. *(ORS 430.399; ORS 426.228)*
2. **Then ask what was offered.** Most refusals are refusals of a specific offer: a congregate bed across town with no pets, no partner, no storage, a curfew, and a sobriety rule. That is not an offer for the person who said no. Record the reason against the offer (site and rule), not against the person, and fix the offer: non-congregate, takes the pet and the partner and the belongings, near the person's community, with a ride and a held bed.
3. **Same small team, back tomorrow.** The evidence for encampment resolution is sustained contact by the same few workers who return repeatedly with a real offer, not a one-time visit with a clipboard. The named lead keeps the case; each attempt is documented; the person stays on the verified list in the presumed band, which is the team's re-find list, never a headcount.
4. **Bring the clinical line to the sidewalk.** A 24/7 consult line tells refusal from untreated psychosis or withdrawal. Where a qualified mental health professional believes the commitment standard is met, they can request a director's hold; since January 2026 that standard reaches foreseeable harm and basic needs, not only imminent danger, and outpatient orders exist. The order is only useful if a bed and a team are waiting at the other end. *(ORS 426.233; ORS 426.131 and 426.133 (HB 2005))*
5. **Never write 'service-refusing'.** The person's row reads contact, no referral. Declines live on the offer, aggregated nightly with no person key, because a dated per-person decline log is prosecution evidence under the city's camping ordinance and a target for data requests. Refusing a service or a data question never lowers the lane or eligibility for any later door; federal coordinated-entry rules require refusal without retribution. *(Portland City Code 14A.50.150; HUD Notice CPD-17-01 II.B.11)*
6. **Enforcement last, and only after a real offer.** The camping ordinance requires that the person was offered and rejected reasonable alternate shelter. So the offer must be real (a bed the person could use, that night, with a ride) and documented at the offer level before any citation or removal. Removal follows a sequence: notice, the outreach lead present, belongings stored and retrievable, medications and documents protected, no removal of someone mid-treatment. Modeling of repeated involuntary displacement of people who inject drugs shows more deaths, so enforcement is a public-order tool with a health cost, not a housing intervention, and every post-removal contact is counted.
7. **Count the no.** Each night's declines by offer and rule, the no-door-open codes by hour, and the re-approach outcomes are published beside the headline count. That is how a refusal stops being a story about the person and becomes a finding about the door.

**How people actually get off the street.** Stripped of slogans, the mechanism is this sequence, and the design counts each step so a failure at any of them is visible.

1. Know who is outside, by name, with a named lead and a next step (stage 3). Identified is not engaged; the county's own count of newly engaged people is the number that has to move.
2. Offer the right first door for the person in front of you (the six questions), not the bed that happens to be open. A first-time family gets cash and a motel, not the chronic queue; a person in withdrawal gets medical care, not sobering; a person in psychosis gets a crisis worker, not a cell.
3. Make the offer real: non-congregate where it matters, takes the pet and the partner and the belongings, with a held bed, a ride, and an arrival written by the receiving site. Most nights the honest answer is that no such door is open, and the design counts that as the missing door, not as a refusal.
4. Hold the person's place in the next stage before they leave this one: the bed after detox, the unit after treatment, the worker before the jail door opens. Transitions are where people are lost.
5. For the highest-acuity few, stabilization first for hours to weeks, then a lease with an ACT team, master leasing, and a risk fund. Roughly a tenth of the people account for half the shelter nights and most of the public-safety contacts; the whole system is judged on whether they stop cycling.
6. Enforcement only after all of that, on the record, with belongings stored, and with the outreach lead present. It moves people; it does not house them, and the count should show where they went.

**What the front line will say.** Before publication an outreach worker, a paramedic, a patrol officer, a jail release desk, an emergency-department social worker, an addiction clinician, and a lawyer were each asked what breaks at 2 a.m. The design changed where they were right (section 9). Their objections are the ones any rollout will hear in its first week, so we keep them here with the answer, by role.
**Outreach workers.**

- *"You are adding data entry to a job that already has forty fields and a separate mapping tool."* At the scene nothing new is written. Two fields at end of shift (referral result and arrival status) join the contact row the handbook already requires, and the county retires the parallel mapping-tool count the same day, so this is one entry instead of two.
- *"A named lead within 7 days is fantasy with six workers for 1,800 verified people."* The rule comes with a caseload ceiling per worker and a visible lead-capacity-exhausted state. When the ceiling is hit the gap is published as staffing, not as your failure. The county cut its ten navigators to zero; the design makes that cut legible.
- *"Yes-to-arrival within 24 hours will make us look like we fail every night."* A yes with no door open at that hour is coded no door open and reported as a capacity finding by door and hour, never as a worker or program score. The metric is red by construction until the sobering and withdrawal center opens, and it is meant to be.
- *"The city's camp teams and police make most of the offers to my caseload and I cannot see them."* City Street Services and police offers write the referral event and result into the shared row, or the yes-to-arrival headline is labeled county-contracted outreach only until they do. A cross-program offer view is part of the named-lead design.
- *"Belongings, storage, and dogs decide more refusals than anything on your list."* Belongings and storage, pets, partner, and community are decline reasons coded against the offer, and storage capacity becomes a counted resource alongside beds and transport.
- *"Some camps I cannot safely approach, and a contact with no offer will look like I did nothing."* A worker-safety code (dog, weapon, unsafe to approach) explains a contact that produced no offer without coding the person. Attempted, not found, and seen, not engaged, are their own states.

**Police.**

- *"We do not have HMIS and never will."* You are not asked to. The record is a dispatch disposition set (offered, declined with reason, transported to, arrival time), matched monthly to the receiving door's admission log. Entering it into the system of record is the receiving site's job.
- *"Sobering will not take the drunk who cannot stand. What do I do at 2 a.m.?"* ORS 430.399 says an incapacitated person goes to a treatment facility or an emergency department; Unity takes intoxication drop-offs for adults 24/7; jail is lawful only when nothing will admit them, and the count records that as a system failure, not the person's. The protocol names those doors and their hours.
- *"I have no authority to make anyone go anywhere below the danger standard."* Correct, and the rules say so in plain words: transport is voluntary unless ORS 426.228 or 430.399 applies. Every use of the word hold in the design means a held bed, not a detention.
- *"If there is a warrant, the person is going to jail whatever your protocol says."* Then the referral result is door refused, charge ineligibility, not the person's decline, and the warrant is never written into the shared record. Fire CHAT does not run warrants, so a CHAT referral to sobering is not blocked by one.
- *"Transport ties up a car for an hour and I have calls holding."* The rule names who transports per door: the referring team for sobering, police or EMS for Unity, an ambulance for an emergency department, a dispatched ride for shelter with the arrival still confirmed by the site. Where no transport exists at that hour the result is coded no transport, so the missing piece is named.
- *"The camping ordinance requires an offer and a refusal. Is this list our evidence?"* No. Declines are recorded against the offer, aggregated, with no person key, and the design carries a written non-disclosure rule for enforcement requests. Enforcement documentation is the city's separate record of a specific, usable offer.

**Paramedics and fire.**

- *"Half of the people your intoxication question describes fail the deflection-center transport criteria."* The question is now two branches with the vital-sign and orientation gate in the observable text: walking, oriented, able to agree goes to sobering; withdrawal signs, unable to walk or agree, or a psychiatric component goes to medical care.
- *"We cannot start buprenorphine on someone who is high, and CHAT's induction program runs weekdays until 5:30."* Induction happens at a prescribing door in withdrawal (Unity, an emergency department, a bridge clinic, Hooper). For someone still intoxicated the protocol says naloxone in hand, a timed re-approach, and a held next-morning induction slot.
- *"Fire apparatus cannot transport to a shelter outside a declared emergency."* The rule names the authorized transporter per destination and allows a dispatched ride with the arrival confirmed by the site. Fire is never asked to transport where its protocol forbids it.
- *"Refusal with capacity is a documented legal disposition and your questions have no branch for it."* Each stabilization question now has a refusal path: document capacity, code the decline against the offer, task a re-contact within 12–24 hours, and push the result to the outreach lead.

**The jail.**

- *"The median stay is about a day. A 72-hour in-reach flag fires after most people are already on the sidewalk."* The living-situation question moves to the booking interview, which recognizance staff already run for every booking around the clock. Stays under 72 hours get a release-day handoff standard; longer stays get a worker.
- *"The Sheriff does not decide who leaves or when. Do not attribute street releases to us."* Discharge-to-street events are attributed by release authority (recognizance, court order or dismissal, bail, time served, emergency release, transfer), and the destination gap is owned at the system level by the body that spans Sheriff, courts, DA, and DCJ.
- *"Release processing takes hours and release times are not given out. A held bed with a 5 p.m. cutoff is useless."* The hold becomes a release window, not a time, and the release notification to the receiving site becomes a counted event; the jail already runs victim notification on the same trigger.
- *"There is no housing field in the booking system, and two of the three record systems are not the county's."* The field is added to the booking record as living situation, so it is not confused with cell classification, collected at the recognizance interview. The design names the Sheriff and the circuit court as the owners the county board does not control.

**Hospitals.**

- *"We have social workers, not housing workers, and no HMIS access."* The in-reach worker is a county worker inside the hospital or the coordinated-care network under a business associate agreement, or is named in an authorization signed at the admission housing question. Hospital staff are not asked to run assessments.
- *"Most of our homeless encounters are treat-and-release emergency visits that end at 3 a.m., never admissions."* An emergency visit without admission is recorded as a contact with a living situation and a referral result and routed to a named lead, the same as an outreach contact. The 72-hour clock applies to admissions only.
- *"Discharge code 01 is the routine code every housed patient gets. It does not mean street."* The design drops the disposition code and builds discharge-to-street from a destination log: named site, bed confirmed, transport provided, time. The federal housing screen you already run at admission is the flag, extended to emergency discharges.
- *"Respite refuses anyone who cannot walk or manage medication, and it takes referrals weekdays 8 to 5. Where does the rest go?"* The respite entry splits into respite-eligible and needs skilled or long-term care, the second on a named clock (eligibility screen, the extended skilled-nursing benefit, guardianship). Referrals declined for criteria are counted separately from those declined for capacity.
- *"The patient who said yes to detox at 2 a.m. sits in our lobby until Hooper opens at 6:45. Is that a failed arrival?"* No. Held in place, with the door and its opening hour named, is a counted interim state; the clock runs until arrival. The one-hour weekday window is published as the door's rule.

**Clinicians.**

- *"Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal kill in days. Never route that to a sobering station."* Withdrawal signs (shaking, drenching sweat, vomiting, confusion, seizure history) route to medical withdrawal management or an emergency department in the protocol's second branch, and alcohol is named as a gap the count does not yet separate.
- *"Methamphetamine psychosis can take weeks to clear, not hours or days."* The rule now says hours to days for intoxication and most withdrawal, days to weeks for stimulant or primary psychosis, with the housing offer made from the stabilization bed as soon as the person can say yes to a room and the lease held by the system where signing capacity is in doubt.
- *"Detox followed by residential treatment without medication raises overdose death from lost tolerance."* Outpatient medication with retention is the default for opioid use; detox and residential are detours taken when indicated and chosen, each with the next bed held; deaths within 30 days of leaving any institution become a counted number. The continuum still lacks an outpatient treatment stage, and the caveats say so.
- *"Oxford Houses vote, and they vote out people on buprenorphine."* The recovery-housing registry records per house whether it accepts residents on medication, and only those beds count as capacity for the substance-use cohorts. Acceptance becomes a condition of county-funded beds.
- *"Cannot consent is a capacity determination. An officer or outreach worker cannot make it."* It is no longer a lane criterion. Impaired tonight is a deferral, re-approached within 12–24 hours; Lane 3 is scored on repeated contacts. The capacity to accept a room is lower than the capacity to sign a lease, which master leasing sidesteps.

**Shelter operators.**

- *"You are grading us on a destination field our guests will not answer at 6 a.m."* An unknown share above 10% flags the site on data before outcomes, and the design funds a check-out conversation as part of the bed-night. The honest finding stands: the worst-reporting sites are the likeliest to be recycling people to the street.
- *"Arrival confirmation and referral source are another form."* They are two fields on the bed-night bill you already submit, not a new form, and they are the only way a 2 a.m. yes becomes visible instead of argued about.
- *"Our decline rules (no pets, sobriety, curfew) are why our building is safe."* Keep them, and publish them as the door's rules so the count can show which lock fails for whom. The fix is funding non-congregate alternatives for the people those rules exclude, not forcing every site to take everyone.

**Housing providers and landlords.**

- *"High-acuity tenants wreck units and drive out other tenants."* Lane 3 comes with master leasing where the system holds the lease, a damage and vacancy fund, a rapid tenancy-response team, and capped volume; a police call triggers the team, not eviction.
- *"A fidelity score punishes our project-based buildings for not being scattered-site."* Fidelity is scored on staffing ratios and tenancy terms, where the outcome evidence is, not on the scattered-site item; the one randomized test of concentration found a staffed congregate building housed people as well as scattered-site.

**Survivor advocates.**

- *"Any domestic-violence row in a shared system endangers survivors."* The first responder records a living situation only, no violence flag, no referral row. The advocate logs the referral in the comparable database and the count receives aggregate flags. Police responders act under their arrest duty; survivor choice governs from the advocate's first contact.

**County data staff.**

- *"The coordinated-entry referral element is not available to outreach projects, and the arrival field does not exist."* Both are local fields on the outreach contact row until the data standard catches up; the design names which HMIS elements are used where and which are additions.
- *"We are mid-migration to a new database and the city's data live somewhere else."* The first years are a migration and a bilateral negotiation; every published trend is a series break and is labeled as one, with old and new series side by side for at least a year.

**People with lived experience.**

- *"A list that chases 90% coverage and tracks who refused is surveillance."* Recording requires consent; a refusal to be recorded becomes an anonymous tally, never a person key. No warrant or immigration-relevant code enters the shared record, a written non-disclosure rule covers enforcement requests, and refusing a service or a question never lowers the lane or eligibility.
- *"Being presumed on a list is not being helped."* The presumed band exists to send someone back to find you; it is the outreach team's re-find list, not a headcount, and the named-lead rule is what turns identified into engaged.

**What happens when a stage fails, in this order.**

1. **Publish it.** The weekly table shows the stage red, with the named owner beside it. Most failures today are invisible, not tolerated; visibility is the first consequence.
2. **A public corrective plan within 60 days.** The owner says what will change, by when, and which number will move. The plan is published where the table is.
3. **Payment follows the data.** A share of the contract payment is withheld until the missing rows (arrivals, destinations, census) are complete. No penalty for outcomes yet; a stage nobody can see cannot be judged.
4. **Payment follows the outcome.** New slots, referrals, beds, or subsidy allocations shift from the provider or door that misses the number to the one that hits it. The shift and its reason are published.
5. **Reassign the stage.** The contract or the role moves to a new owner, and the board that made the decision says why in public. This is the rare step; the four before it are meant to make it rare.

Never counted as the worker's or the program's failure:

- A yes with no door open at that hour (coded no door open; a capacity finding against the budget).
- A named-lead rule missed because the caseload ceiling was hit (a staffing finding).
- A refusal by someone with capacity, coded against the offer (an offer-quality finding).
- A count that falls because verification capacity was cut (published beside coverage and reliability, so it reads as a cut).

**Who enforces, and what they hold.**

- **The county board:** The SHS dollars and every county contract: bed-night terms, payment-for-data, the assessment and navigator budgets it cut.
- **The city council:** Portland Street Response, city shelters and alternative sites, camping enforcement, and the city-county agreement that decides whether city offers enter the shared row.
- **The SHS Regional Policy and Oversight Committee:** The regional table and the three counties' shared standards; the only body that can make a tri-county number mean the same thing in each county.
- **The Sheriff and the circuit court:** The booking question, the release notification, and the recognizance interview; elected and independent of the board.
- **The Oregon Health Authority, the coordinated care organizations, and the Legislature:** Licensing and staffed-bed reporting, treatment and respite payment, and the benefits the state has so far only studied.
- **The public:** The nine headline numbers and the weekly table. Nothing above works if the table is not published.

1. The unit costs the region does publish all point the same way. A shelter bed costs about three times what supportive housing costs per person ($47,000 against $16,000), and the stages cut in FY2026 (assessment, navigators, rapid rehousing) are the cheap conversion steps between the two.
2. Most stage costs are unpublished. Capacity comes with the bill in this design, so for eight of the fourteen stages the first deliverable is a cost per admission, per placement, or per exit, not a new appropriation.
3. Now: restore the defunded doors and build the missing stages that are cheap (a cash pool, a registry, in-reach staff, a cohort table, a destination log). These are staff and rules, not buildings.
4. Later: shift shelter money toward exits and supportive housing as the front of the continuum works. The large capital items (withdrawal, residential, crisis-residential beds) are state and payer money; the county's job is to publish the need and hold the next bed.
5. The 2026 turnaround proposal's lane budgets (roughly $70–100M, $70–120M, and $40–80M a year for the three lanes, adding to about the flat SHS envelope) are a candidate's arithmetic on unmeasured unit costs. They are useful as a shape and are labeled as an assumption.

## 14. What we could not verify, and the records requests that would settle it

- **The county's inactivity threshold.** No fetched county page states the number of days after which a person leaves the active list. Request to HSD: the written inactive policy the Built for Zero scorecard says exists.
- **Provider coverage of the by-name list.** The scorecard says fewer than 90% of providers report. Request: the list of contracted providers and which ones submit to HMIS, with last-submission dates.
- **Facility-level bed counts and occupancy** for Hooper, residential treatment, recovery housing, and the Behavioral Health Resource Center. Hooper's 65 beds is a directory figure, not a primary one. Request to the county and OHA: licensed, staffed, and occupied beds by facility, monthly.
- **Jail release destinations.** Request to the Sheriff: releases by month with housing status at booking, and whether any recurring match to HMIS has run since 2018.
- **Hospital discharge destinations** for patients flagged as homeless at Unity and the four major systems. This may require the hospitals' cooperation rather than a records request.
- **Meieran's 2023 county page** on homelessness and her January 2024 letter to the Governor. Both were removed from the county site and the archive was rate-limited during this research; the contents are known from contemporaneous press coverage only.
- **Recovery-housing inventory.** No public total exists for Oxford Houses or county-funded recovery beds beyond the $5.05M program list.
